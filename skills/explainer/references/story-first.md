# Story first

The story decides everything else: what is on screen, what moves, and what the voice-over says. Write it before making any visuals.

## Find the one idea

1. Write down everything the topic could cover.
2. Pick the one thing a non-technical viewer most needs to understand. The rest goes into other films or is left out.
3. Write that idea as a single plain sentence a viewer could repeat to a friend. This is the takeaway.
4. Test it: if the sentence needs "and", there are probably two films.

Keep a list of the ideas you cut. They are often the next episodes.

## Pick a metaphor

A good metaphor:

- is something the audience already knows from daily life
- behaves the way the real thing behaves, so the film can follow it without breaking it
- can be drawn simply and recognised in a second
- can stretch across a series, so later films can extend it rather than start again

Write down where the metaphor breaks. Either avoid those points, or say on screen where it stops being accurate.

## Write beats

A beat is one step in the explanation. For each beat, write:

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Voice-over | The line, in plain words. Draft only; it is rewritten against the locked picture. |
| On screen | What is visible. |
| Moves | What moves, and what that movement explains. |

Guidelines:

- One new piece of information per beat.
- Every movement must explain something. If you cannot say what a movement explains, remove it.
- Each beat should follow from the one before. If a viewer would ask "why are we looking at this?", a beat is missing.
- End on the takeaway, shown as well as said.

## Example: the forgetful uncle series

A series of short AI explainers for non-technical people. The central metaphor is an uncle who only communicates by letter and remembers nothing between replies. Each episode adds one piece to the same metaphor.

| Episode | Idea | How the metaphor shows it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One page | A model reads what you send and writes a reply. | You post the uncle a one-page letter; he reads it and writes back. |
| Many pages | A longer prompt gives the model more to work from. | You send several pages, and his reply draws on all of them. |
| Tokens | Models read text in small pieces, not whole words or pages. | The uncle reads the letter in small cut-out pieces, one at a time. |
| The uncle | The model has no memory between replies. | Each time a letter arrives, he has forgotten every earlier one. To carry on a conversation, you send the whole history again. |
| The letter limit | The context window is a limit on how much the model can read at once. | The uncle can only read so many pages per letter. Past that, the earliest pages are left out. |
| The filing cabinet | Skills and tools: the model sees a short index and asks for folders when it needs them. | The uncle has a filing cabinet index. He reads only the index, then writes back asking for the folders he needs, which you send in the next letter. |

### Sample beats from "The uncle"

Takeaway: *The model does not remember your last message; the whole conversation is sent again each time.*

| # | Voice-over (draft) | On screen | Moves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | "You write to your uncle." | A letter on a desk. | The letter slides into an envelope: a message is being sent. |
| 2 | "He reads it and writes back." | The uncle at his desk. | His reply travels back across the frame: the response. |
| 3 | "Then he forgets the whole thing." | The uncle's desk. | His desk empties: nothing is kept. |
| 4 | "So you send the next letter with all the earlier ones stapled on." | A stack of pages. | The stack grows with each new letter: the full history is resent. |
| 5 | "That's how the conversation keeps going." | The growing stack beside a short reply. | Stack and reply sit side by side: the takeaway, shown. |
